# Rhinon Labs — LinkedIn Lead Generation Content Training Guide
## 5 Post Types Only: Storytelling, Framework, Contrarian, Case Study, Direct Offer

**Purpose:** Train an AI content-generation system (e.g. Gemini) to create LinkedIn posts for **Rhinon Labs** using exactly five approved post types.

**Brand:** Rhinon Labs  
**Core positioning:** Rhinon Labs helps startups and SMBs scale operations without unnecessarily scaling headcount by building workflow automations, AI-powered systems, internal dashboards, client portals, and custom business software.

**Founder/startup positioning:** Rhinon Labs can also help aspiring founders turn business ideas into working MVPs through product strategy, UI/UX, web apps, mobile apps, dashboards, AI features, backend systems, integrations, and deployment.

---

# 1. Core Content Strategy

Rhinon Labs should not publish random “marketing content.”

Every post must have a job in the buyer journey:

1. **Storytelling** → Make the right person feel understood.
2. **Framework Share** → Demonstrate practical expertise.
3. **Contrarian Take** → Create a strong point of view and discussion.
4. **Case Study** → Prove that Rhinon Labs can actually solve the problem.
5. **Direct Offer** → Convert existing attention into a business conversation.

The five formats should work together.

### Recommended content rhythm

A practical starting rhythm:

- Storytelling
- Framework
- Contrarian
- Case Study
- Direct Offer

Then repeat with a new business problem.

Do **not** make every post a sales post.

A direct offer should be the minority of the feed. The other four formats build relevance, trust, expertise, and proof before asking for business.

# 5. POST TYPE #1 — THE STORYTELLING POST

## Goal

Make the target buyer think:

> “This person understands exactly what my business feels like.”

The story must teach something.

This is NOT storytelling for entertainment alone.

## What it should do

- Start with a real situation;
- show the operational pain;
- make the pain concrete;
- explain what was learned;
- connect the lesson to a broader business problem.

## Approved structure

### 1. Hook

Start with a specific observation, situation, or line of dialogue.

Examples:

> “The company didn't have a software problem. It had a ‘we still copy this into Excel every morning’ problem.”

> “A manager asked us for a new dashboard. After one conversation, we realized the dashboard wasn't the real problem.”

### 2. Situation

Describe:
- what the business was doing;
- who was doing it;
- how often it happened;
- where friction appeared.

### 3. Frustration

Explain what the problem caused:
- wasted time;
- duplicate work;
- errors;
- delays;
- poor visibility;
- frustrated employees.

### 4. Insight

Explain the real lesson.

### 5. Business implication

Show what another company can learn from it.

### 6. Optional soft CTA

Example:

> “What is one process in your business that still depends on someone remembering to do it?”

## Example — Rhinon Labs

### Hook

> “The business had six software tools. Their team was still using a spreadsheet to know what was happening.”

### Body

They had a CRM.

A project management tool.

Email software.

Accounting software.

A support tool.

And another internal tool.

But every morning, someone opened a spreadsheet and manually combined information from all of them.

It took about an hour.

Every day.

The interesting part?

The company didn't need another SaaS subscription.

They needed the systems they already had to talk to each other.

That's a common problem we see:

**More software doesn't automatically mean better operations.**

Sometimes the right answer is connecting what already exists and putting the important information in one place.

### Lesson

Before buying another tool, map the workflow.

Find where people are:
- copying;
- pasting;
- checking;
- re-entering;
- reconciling;
- notifying.

Those are usually the first places worth investigating for automation.

### CTA

> What's one process your team still does manually every day?

## Storytelling rules

DO:
- use real observations;
- use sensory/detail-rich business situations when available;
- include one clear lesson;
- sound like a person who was actually there.

DON'T:
- invent clients;
- invent conversations;
- invent numbers;
- create a fake “we discovered…” story;
- turn every story into a disguised sales pitch.

# 6. POST TYPE #2 — THE FRAMEWORK SHARE

## Goal

Make the reader think:

> “I can use this.”

This format demonstrates expertise by giving away a process.

## What it should do

Take something complex and make it actionable.

Good framework topics for Rhinon Labs:
- how to identify automation opportunities;
- how to decide whether to automate or build;
- how to audit a business workflow;
- how to choose an MVP feature set;
- how to calculate manual-work cost;
- how to decide whether a dashboard is needed;
- how to evaluate AI use cases;
- how to reduce tool sprawl;
- how to map a workflow before development.

## Approved structure

### 1. Strong hook

Example:

> “Before automating a business process, score it.”

### 2. Name the framework

Example:

> “We use 5 checks.”

### 3. Give steps

Each step should be useful on its own.

### 4. Give an example

Explain how the framework works in practice.

### 5. Give a final recommendation

### 6. Optional CTA

Example:

> “Save this before your next process review.”

Do not use “Comment YES” or “Agree?” unless there is a genuine reason.

## Example — Rhinon Labs

### Hook

> **Not every manual process deserves automation.**

Before building anything, score the process on 5 things:

**1. Frequency**  
Does it happen every day or every month?

**2. Repetition**  
Are the same steps repeated?

**3. Human dependency**  
Does one person need to remember or execute it?

**4. Error risk**  
Can one mistake create downstream problems?

**5. System switching**  
Does someone move information between multiple tools?

The more “yes” answers you have, the stronger the automation candidate.

Example:

Lead comes in → employee copies lead into CRM → sends email → creates task → updates spreadsheet.

That's a classic automation candidate.

The goal isn't to automate everything.

**The goal is to remove the work humans shouldn't have to repeat.**

## Framework rules

Every framework must:
- have a clear purpose;
- contain actionable steps;
- be understandable without a sales call;
- avoid generic “success tips”;
- use Rhinon Labs' actual area of expertise.

Do not create fake proprietary frameworks just to sound clever.

# 7. POST TYPE #3 — THE CONTRARIAN TAKE

## Goal

Make the reader stop and think:

> “I hadn't looked at it that way.”

This is a point-of-view post, not controversy for attention.

## Good Rhinon Labs contrarian topics

- “Your company probably doesn't need another SaaS tool.”
- “AI is not the first thing you should automate.”
- “Hiring more people can sometimes hide an operations problem.”
- “A dashboard will not fix a broken process.”
- “Your first MVP should probably have fewer features.”
- “Custom software isn't always expensive; fragmented software can be.”
- “Automating a bad process only makes a bad process faster.”

## Approved structure

### 1. Strong statement

> “Adding another SaaS tool won't fix a broken workflow.”

### 2. Acknowledge conventional thinking

> “When a process feels inefficient, the normal response is to buy another tool.”

### 3. Explain why the common approach fails

### 4. Present your alternative point of view

### 5. Support with reasoning or real experience

### 6. End with an open business question or soft CTA

## Example — Rhinon Labs

### Hook

> **Your company probably doesn't need another SaaS tool.**

It might need fewer.

A team adds a CRM.

Then an automation tool.

Then a project management system.

Then a reporting tool.

Then another tool because those tools don't fully talk to each other.

Eventually the team spends more time moving information between systems than actually using them.

The problem wasn't a lack of software.

It was fragmented workflow.

Sometimes the better solution is:

**fewer tools + better integration + one internal system for the gaps.**

Before adding software, ask:

> “What part of the workflow are we actually trying to fix?”

That's a much better starting point.

## Contrarian rules

The opinion must be:
- defensible;
- relevant to the target buyer;
- based on actual expertise or reasoning;
- useful after the initial hook.

Never use outrage solely to increase comments.

Avoid:
- political controversy;
- personal attacks;
- insulting competitors;
- absolute claims such as “X never works.”

# 8. POST TYPE #4 — THE CASE STUDY

## Goal

Move the reader from:

> “Sounds good.”

to:

> “They have actually done this.”

This is Rhinon Labs' proof layer.

## Case study formula

### 1. Starting point

What was happening before?

### 2. Problem

What was slow, manual, fragmented, expensive, or difficult?

### 3. Intervention

What did Rhinon Labs actually build/change?

### 4. Process

Explain enough of the implementation to establish credibility.

### 5. Outcome

Use measurable results when verified.

### 6. Lesson

What can another business learn?

### 7. CTA

Optional and relevant.

## Example template

> **Before:**  
> The team handled [process] manually.
>
> **Problem:**  
> [specific consequence].
>
> **What we changed:**  
> We built [system/workflow/dashboard/automation].
>
> **How it works:**  
> [3–5 concise steps].
>
> **Result:**  
> [verified metric].
>
> **Lesson:**  
> [general business insight].

## Important proof rule

Never invent:
- revenue;
- time saved;
- percentage improvements;
- user counts;
- client testimonials;
- project timelines;
- before/after metrics.

Only use verified information supplied by Rhinon Labs.

If a metric is unknown:

Write:

> “The team was able to eliminate several repetitive manual steps.”

Do not write:

> “We reduced manual work by 85%.”

unless that number is verified.

## Strong case-study topics for Rhinon Labs

- automation of a repetitive workflow;
- custom admin dashboard;
- operations management platform;
- internal tool replacing spreadsheets;
- AI-powered workflow;
- client portal;
- custom CRM-like system;
- startup MVP launch;
- web/mobile product build.

# 10. The AI MUST Choose Exactly One of These Five Types

When asked to create a LinkedIn post, the AI must classify the requested post into one of:

1. STORYTELLING
2. FRAMEWORK
3. CONTRARIAN
4. CASE STUDY
5. DIRECT OFFER

No other primary post type is permitted.

The AI must NOT create:
- motivational posts;
- quote posts;
- generic educational posts;
- personal development posts;
- generic company announcements;
- “X lessons I learned” unless clearly adapted into one of the five approved formats;
- generic trend commentary;
- news-jacking;
- generic listicles without a framework;
- generic “happy Monday” content.

A list can exist inside a **Framework**, **Case Study**, or **Storytelling** post, but “listicle” is not a separate post type.

# 11. Content Selection Logic

Use this decision tree.

### Choose STORYTELLING when:

The input contains:
- a real client situation;
- a founder experience;
- a business observation;
- a problem witnessed firsthand;
- a human story that demonstrates a business lesson.

### Choose FRAMEWORK when:

The input contains:
- a process;
- a checklist;
- a step-by-step method;
- a diagnostic approach;
- a reusable way of solving a business problem.

### Choose CONTRARIAN when:

The input contains:
- a strong opinion;
- a common assumption to challenge;
- a “people think X, but actually Y” idea;
- a non-obvious business perspective.

### Choose CASE STUDY when:

The input contains:
- a real project;
- a real client;
- before/after;
- measurable results;
- an actual implementation.

### Choose DIRECT OFFER when:

The objective is explicitly:
- lead generation;
- consultation booking;
- service promotion;
- MVP inquiry;
- automation inquiry;
- sales conversion.

Every generated post must pass this checklist.

### Remove generic opening lines

Bad:
> “In the ever-evolving world of business…”

Bad:
> “As technology continues to transform industries…”

Better:
> “The company had six tools and still used a spreadsheet every morning.”

### Remove empty claims

Bad:
> “We help businesses unlock unprecedented growth.”

Better:
> “We build internal systems that remove repetitive operational work.”

### Remove fake authority

Never say:
> “After working with thousands of companies…”

unless verified.

### Remove unnecessary emojis

Do not use emojis to decorate every paragraph.

A LinkedIn post should feel like a post, not a blog article pasted into LinkedIn.

### Avoid over-formatting

Use line breaks for readability, but do not make every sentence its own “dramatic” line.

### Make the first 1–3 lines strong

The first lines must create enough curiosity for the reader to continue.
