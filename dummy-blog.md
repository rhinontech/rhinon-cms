Every founder we meet says the same thing: "I just need a developer." What they actually need is a **decision framework**. The difference between an MVP that ships in 30 days and one that drags on for six months is almost never engineering talent — it is the discipline to decide what *not* to build.

At Rhinon Labs we have taken dozens of products from a napkin sketch to a live URL. This is the exact playbook we run, week by week.

## Week 1: Scope Like Your Runway Depends on It

Because it does. The first week is not about code — it is about deletion. Take your feature list and cut it three times.

- **Cut one:** remove everything a spreadsheet or a manual process could do for the first 100 users.
- **Cut two:** remove everything that only matters at scale — admin dashboards, role systems, analytics.
- **Cut three:** remove everything you added because a competitor has it.

What survives is your **core loop**: the single action a user takes, the value they get back, and the reason they return. If you cannot describe your product in one sentence built around that loop, you are not done cutting.

> An MVP is not a smaller version of your product. It is the fastest experiment that proves someone will pay for it.

## Week 2: Build the Boring Stack

Founders lose weeks debating frameworks. Do not. Pick tools that are **boring, documented, and hireable** — Next.js on the front, Postgres behind an API, and one payment provider. Novel technology is a tax you pay every single day after launch.

Two rules keep Week 2 honest:

1. **No custom infrastructure.** Managed hosting, managed database, managed auth. You can migrate later; you cannot un-waste three weeks of DevOps.
2. **One environment promotion path.** A staging branch and a production branch. Anything fancier is procrastination wearing a lanyard.

## Week 3: Demo Every Friday or It Did Not Happen

The single highest-leverage habit in product development is the **weekly demo**. Not a status update — a live walkthrough of the actual product, in a browser, with real clicks.

Demos force integration. Bugs that hide in branches for weeks surface in minutes when someone tries to complete the core loop end to end. They also force honesty: "90% done" evaporates the moment a signup form throws an error in front of an audience.

Invite a real target user to at least one demo. Watching a stranger hesitate on your pricing page teaches you more than a month of internal debate.

## Week 4: Launch Before You Feel Ready

You will not feel ready. Ship anyway. The last week is a checklist, not a sprint:

- Error tracking wired up and alerting to a channel someone actually reads
- A payment path tested with a real card
- One onboarding email, written like a human wrote it
- Analytics on exactly three events: signup, activation, payment

Everything else — SEO polish, dark mode, the settings page — belongs in the backlog. Your first ten users care about one thing: does the core loop deliver the value you promised?

## The Real Secret: Momentum Compounds

Thirty days is not a gimmick. It is a forcing function. Teams that ship in a month develop a metabolism — decide fast, build small, learn weekly — that compounds into a durable advantage long after launch. Teams that "take the time to do it right" usually discover, six months later, that they built the wrong thing very carefully.

Scope ruthlessly. Build boring. Demo weekly. Launch scared. That is the whole playbook — and it fits in 30 days.
